Electricity is water in pipes

Voltage is pressure, current is flow, resistance is a narrow pipe, and ground is the return pipe. Current only moves around a complete loop. Break it anywhere and everything stops.

Loop complete: current flows
  • This is exactly the bug your board had: J1's ground pins weren't connected, so 5V came in with no way back.
  • "5V" always means 5 volts higher than ground. Ground is the zero point everything is measured from.

USB-C pins, and why they come in pairs

The connector has a top row (A) and a bottom row (B). Row B is the same as row A rotated 180°, so the cable works either way up. That's why you tie A6 to B6 and A7 to B7.

The CC handshake: "I'm a device, send me 5V"

A USB-C charger keeps VBUS off until it sees a device. It pushes a little current out on CC through its own resistor (Rp). Your 5.1k resistor (Rd) pulls that wire toward ground, making a voltage divider. The charger reads the voltage in the middle.

0.42 V: device found, VBUS turns ON
  • Each CC pin gets its own resistor because only one is used, depending on which way the cable is plugged in.
  • No resistor means the charger reads about 5V, decides nothing is there, and never turns power on.
  • 5.1k is the value in the USB-C spec. Math: 5V × 5.1 / (56 + 5.1) ≈ 0.42V.

Regulator and capacitors

The ESP32 runs on 3.3V and 5V would damage it. U1 works like a pressure-reducing valve: 5V in, a steady 3.3V out, with the extra burned off as a little heat. Capacitors are small water tanks that cover sudden demand.

  • Pins: VIN gets 5V, VOUT gives 3.3V, GND is its reference, EN is tied to VIN so it's always on.
  • Swapped from the XC6206 (200 mA) because Wi-Fi pulls bursts up to about 500 mA.
  • Without the output capacitor the regulator can oscillate, and the ESP32 crashes or glitches.

What the ESP32 needs around it

  • C3 (10µF) and C4 (100nF) sit right at the 3V3 pin. The big one covers slow dips, the small one reacts to tiny fast spikes.
  • Strapping pins IO0, IO3, IO45, IO46 are read at startup to choose the boot mode. Keep sensors off them.
  • IO35-IO37 are used for extra memory (PSRAM) on some module versions. Leave them alone.
  • USB_D+ and USB_D- go straight to pins 14 and 13. The ESP32-S3 has USB built in.

Labels are walkie-talkie channels

A label connects to every other label with the exact same name, with no wire drawn between them. One label alone connects to nothing, which is the "Label connected to only one pin" warning.

  • Power symbols (GND, +3V3, VBUS) work the same way: every GND symbol is one big shared wire.
  • A capacitor touching a pin does not put that pin on a power net. The pin still needs its own GND or +3V3 symbol.

Symbol → footprint → pads

Every part exists twice. The symbol is the drawing in the schematic. The footprint is the group of copper pads on the board. Pin 1 becomes pad 1, pin 2 becomes pad 2. If the numbers don't match, the board is wired wrong even when the schematic looks perfect.

  • "0805" is a size code: 0.08 × 0.05 inches, about a grain of rice. Big enough to hand-solder.
  • SMD pads are flat squares on the surface. Through-hole pads are copper rings around a drilled hole (headers use these).
  • The ESP32 module has 41 pads. Pad 41 is a big square underneath for ground and heat.

Pin headers

Header pins are metal posts you snap off a 40-pin strip and solder into a row of through-hole pads. Jumper wires, breadboards, and modules plug onto them. Male and female versions fit the same holes, so the footprint doesn't lock you in.
